Role 3: The LLM as the prediction model itself

The third role is the one people usually have in mind when they ask whether AI has changed predictive analytics. Instead of training a model, you write a prompt:

Here is a student: 9th grade, 22 absences, 1.8 GPA, two disciplinary referrals, eligible for free lunch. What is the probability this student does not graduate on time?

The LLM will answer. It will produce a number, it will sound confident, and the number may not be unreasonable.

This is technically possible and, for most public service applications, usually the wrong choice. Being able to explain why is worth more than knowing the conclusion — so this page walks through the reasons, and then through the narrower cases where the answer flips.

Why this is usually the wrong tool for risk scoring

The probabilities are not calibrated, and cannot easily be made so. Now that you have worked through calibration, you can see the problem clearly. An LLM’s “0.7” is a plausible-sounding token, not an estimate from a distribution. There is no reason to expect that among everyone the model calls 0.7, about 70% experience the outcome, and in practice these numbers cluster on round values and shift with wording. For any of the uses where calibration matters — capacity planning, cost-benefit reasoning, communicating a number to a caseworker — this is disqualifying.

You cannot audit it. A logistic regression has coefficients. A random forest has variable importance and a describable procedure. When a family asks why they were flagged, or a journalist files a records request, or a court asks how the system reached a decision, you need an answer. “The model produced this output and we do not know from what” is not one. For high-stakes public decisions, this alone usually ends the conversation.

The training data are unknown and unauditable. Every bias analysis in this course rests on knowing something about where your data came from. With a general-purpose LLM you know essentially nothing about the corpus behind the predictions, which means you cannot reason about whose patterns the model absorbed. You can still measure disparities in the outputs — and you should — but you cannot explain or fix their source.

The model changes underneath you. Providers update models continuously, often without changing the name you call, and retire versions on their own schedule. A predictive system whose behavior can silently change on a vendor’s timeline is very difficult to monitor, and directly at odds with everything on the deployment page. A model you trained yourself sits in a file and does the same thing tomorrow.

Prompt sensitivity. Reorder the predictors, rephrase the question, change the units, and the number moves. A model whose output depends on incidental formatting choices is not a stable measurement instrument.

Benchmark contamination makes published comparisons unreliable. When you read that an LLM performed impressively on some prediction task, ask whether that dataset was on the public internet before the model was trained. For most well-known benchmark datasets it was, so the model may be recalling rather than predicting. This is why your own held-out test data — data the model provably could not have seen — is the only evidence that settles the question.

Cost and latency. An API call per prediction, at scale, is orders of magnitude more expensive and slower than scoring rows with a fitted model. For a one-time analysis this is irrelevant; for a system scoring every new referral it is not.

Where it is actually reasonable

The case against LLM-as-predictor is specific to risk scoring on structured data in high-stakes settings. There are real situations where reaching for one makes sense:

  • You have no training data at all. Every method in this course requires historical cases with known outcomes. If a program is brand new, you have nothing to train on, and an LLM’s general knowledge may beat nothing — as a stopgap, explicitly labeled as such, while you build the data infrastructure to do it properly.

  • The task is genuinely about language. Classifying what a case note is about, routing an inquiry to the right department, or summarizing themes across open-ended responses are tasks where language understanding is the job. This is closer to Role 2 than to risk scoring.

  • The stakes are low and the volume is small. Not every prediction problem is a consequential decision about a person.

  • As a source of hypotheses. Asking what predictors are worth considering for a given outcome can be a useful starting point for a conversation with subject-matter experts — as long as the answers are then tested rather than believed.

If you do it anyway, evaluate it identically

Here is the part that matters most, and it is the reason this page belongs in a course rather than a policy memo.

If a colleague or a vendor proposes an LLM-based risk score, you do not need to argue about it in the abstract. You already have the machinery to settle it. An LLM-based predictor is just another learner, and it earns its place exactly the way every other learner does:

  • Run it on your held-out test data, the same data used for every other learner.
  • Compute the same metrics — AUC-ROC, AUC-PR, and the threshold-variant metrics at your chosen threshold.
  • Compute the Brier score and a calibration plot. This is where these systems usually come apart.
  • Run the same bias analyses across the same subgroups.
  • Compare it against your benchmark learner — the simple, transparent, explainable one.

Then apply the standard this course has used throughout: added complexity must earn its way in. An LLM-based predictor is not merely more complex than a logistic regression; it is less auditable, less stable, more expensive, and harder to govern. The performance improvement required to justify all of that is correspondingly large — and in published comparisons on structured prediction tasks, it is usually not there.

Being able to say “we tested it, here are the numbers, here is the calibration plot, and it did not beat our benchmark” is a far stronger professional position than either uncritical enthusiasm or reflexive dismissal. Learning to say a well-evidenced no is a real skill.
